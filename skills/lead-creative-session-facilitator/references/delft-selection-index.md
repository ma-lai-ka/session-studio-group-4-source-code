# Delft selection index

Read the whole index when screening Delft candidates, alongside [the catalogue](catalogue.md) for models, approaches and perspectives. Categories aid retrieval; they do not prescribe a fixed order or exclude cross-phase use.

## Meaning and limits

- **Purpose, inputs, outputs and cautions below are editorial screening summaries**, drawn from existing recovered source records. They support comparison; they are not complete DDG instructions, exact source wording or universal eligibility rules. Timing, difficulty and team-size defaults from the website have deliberately not been imported.
- **R**: an existing reviewed procedure summary is available; this does not mean the current assistant has read the source or that every claim has been re-audited. **G**: a procedure/instrument gap remains. The per-entry locator check only verifies retrieval, not the entire method's accuracy or feasibility.
- All PDF locators are **one-based positions in the bundled [Delft scan](books/delft-design-guide.pdf)**, not printed page numbers. Read complete relevant entries and any associated illustrations for shortlisted candidates, including How, Tips & Concerns and Limitations. Do not calculate a global page offset. If a source cannot be read, disclose the gap and do not invent its procedure.
- The eight [starter records](delft-activities.md) remain useful for detail, but are not a selection boundary. Use the [comparison and adaptation workflow](activity-selection.md) before scheduling anything.
- Cite exact book entry and source step/heading in delivered plans. Keep the internal PDF locators here for retrieval; do not copy page numbers into the user-facing plan.

## Methods

### Discover

| Method | Purpose | Required inputs / access | Expected output | Constraints to check | Source locator / status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Contextmapping | Surface everyday experiences and translate them into design insights. | Research topic, participant access and an open-ended sensitising package. | Experience artefacts and interpreted user insights | The source suggests 3–20 participating users; this is a research sample, not a limit on the design team. Sensitising homework happens before the generative encounter. | PDF 75; R |
| Thing Ethnography | Reveal relations around connected objects that human accounts may miss. | Research context, relevant objects and feasible data collection tools. | Data-supported relationships and use patterns | Data volume is not insight. Some settings rule out cameras; specialist analytics may need expert support. Instrumentation does not automatically make interpretations objective. | PDF 77; R |
| Cultural Probes | Gather self-documented experiences to inspire subsequent design research. | A topic, intended users and ways to return completed probe materials. | Returned self-documentation and research leads | Probes inspire possibilities; they do not validate causal explanations or answer every specific research question. The source suggests a few users up to about 30. | PDF 79; R |
| User Observations | Turn observed use into documented themes and design implications. | Research questions, participant access and a real or simulated use setting. | Observed-use records, themes and implications | Being observed can change behaviour. Protect participant privacy and follow the study’s ethical requirements. | PDF 81; R |
| Interviews | Understand participants’ perceptions and motivations through qualitative accounts. | Research questions and access to people familiar with the topic. | Participant accounts and supported interpretations | The source describes roughly one hour per interview, with recruitment and analysis additional. Qualitative interview counts are not the number of designers in a planning session. | PDF 83; R |
| Questionnaires | Collect structured answers to defined research questions. | Research questions, appropriate respondents and an analysis approach. | Responses and analysis answering the research questions | Read Tips & Concerns: qualitative open questions are also allowed. Check burden, respondent access and piloting; sampling limits conclusions. | PDF 85; R |
| Focus Groups | Compare perspectives through moderated discussion with intended users. | A topic guide and participants from the target group. | Range of opinions with contextual quotations | Source guidance: 6–8 target participants plus a moderator and recorder; each group takes 90–120 minutes. The book recommends at least three groups. These numbers concern research groups, not design-team size. | PDF 87; R |
| Ecodesign Strategy Wheel | Locate promising environmental improvements across a product’s life cycle. | A product or concept and evidence about its life-cycle impacts. | Qualitative impact profile and improvement priorities | Scores are qualitative judgments; a spider diagram does not make them objective. Consider behaviour as well as technical changes. | PDF 89; R |
| Ecodesign Checklist | Identify environmental design priorities across the product life cycle. | Product or concept and the Ecodesign Checklist. | Life-cycle issues and improvement options | Obtain the actual checklist before facilitating. The source gives this workflow rather than reproducing every checklist question. Research must be available before scoring; checklists may need contextual additions. | PDF 91; R |
| Fast Track Life Cycle Analysis | Compare environmental burdens and identify life-cycle hotspots. | Product-system inventory, functional performance and appropriate impact data. | Impact estimates, hotspots and uncertainty | Needs suitable impact data and LCA competence. Account for missing data; a short activity does not certify an LCA. | PDF 93; R |
| SWOT & Search Areas | Translate organisational strengths and external opportunities into design search areas. | Evidence about the organisation, users, competitors and external trends. | Innovation search areas and researched design briefs | The source suggests about 20–60 search areas. A two-dimensional matrix simplifies a complex context. | PDF 95; R |
| Trend Foresight | Organise weak signals into an explicit set of future-facing trend topics. | A research domain and collected images/signals from multiple sources. | Named trend topics with supporting signals | Collection and synthesis need separate time. Source suggests about 80 images and around two hours for online search. | PDF 97; R |
| Brand DNA | Describe a coherent purpose, personality and positioning for a brand. | Knowledge of the organisation, its beliefs and intended audience. | Purpose, personality and positioning description | The source treats this as an iterative model, not a fixed sequence. Competition, roots and evidence for values may require additional analysis. | PDF 99; R |
| WWWH | Question and reframe an initial design problem systematically. | An initial problem statement or design brief. | Revised problem statement and information gaps | The indexed label WWWH is retained for compatibility; the scanned heading is WWWWWH. The questions are a checklist, not proof that a problem is understood. | PDF 102; R |
| Design Drawing to Discover | Use sketches to uncover relationships and clarify the brief. | An initial brief, observed situation or product to explore. | Exploratory sketches and questions about the brief | Facilitator grouping of the source’s non-numbered guidance. Keep informal sketches focused on the relevant issue; polish is not the aim. | PDF 104; R |

### Define

| Method | Purpose | Required inputs / access | Expected output | Constraints to check | Source locator / status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Persona | Make research-based user archetypes that inform design decisions. | Rich qualitative research about intended users. | Research-based user archetypes | Personas should represent evidence, not invented stereotypes. Research collection precedes the estimated synthesis workshop. | PDF 108; R |
| Cultura | Structure contextual research to build understanding across cultural boundaries. | A research context, access to participants, Cultura Question Card Set and Canvas. | Contextual findings on the Cultura Canvas | Requires the actual Cultura toolkit; the supplied page explains its use but does not reproduce all cards. A broad context and fine-grained attention may be needed. | PDF 110; R |
| Problem Definition | Agree a clear problem scope and desired situation. | The problem owner’s formulation or findings from problem analysis. | Justified scope and desired situation | Problem definition is iterative; defining a problem does not solve it. | PDF 112; R |
| Function Analysis | Describe what a product and its parts must do before choosing solutions. | A product purpose and information about use and flows. | Function structure and relationships | Function structures are iterative. Describe required functions before committing to physical parts. | PDF 114; R |
| Product Life Cycle | Build a life-cycle activity overview that supports requirements. | A defined product, product group or service. | Life-cycle activities supporting criteria | This is an overview of processes and activities, not a numerical environmental life-cycle assessment. | PDF 116; R |
| List of Requirements | Maintain explicit, usable criteria for developing and comparing designs. | Design brief and evidence about users, context, constraints and standards. | Structured, reviewable demands and wishes | Do not invent regulatory thresholds. Missing evidence or standards must be researched before treating a requirement as validated. | PDF 118; R |
| Mind Mapping | Expose associations and relationships around a central topic. | A theme or design problem. | Map of associations and relationships | Individual mapping before group discussion follows the source guidance. A map structures thinking; it is not independent evidence. | PDF 120; R |
| Journey Mapping | Show user experiences over time and identify opportunities between touchpoints. | Qualitative user evidence and a defined user perspective. | Evidence-based experience map and opportunities | Use research for current experiences, not team assumptions. Quantitative service evidence can support prioritisation where available. | PDF 123; R |
| Product Journey Mapping | Plan product, component and service flows across repeated use cycles. | A product concept, component lifetimes and intended circular service model. | Repeated-use flows and responsible actors | Component lifetime estimates require evidence. This map centres on the product, while a customer journey centres on human experience. | PDF 124; R |
| Project Value Modelling | Align value creation, value capture and project collaboration. | A specific project and the project-value blueprint. | Project-value model and collaboration choices | Requires the project-value blueprint referenced in the source. It targets project-specific value tensions rather than every firm-level strategy. | PDF 126; R |
| Business Modelling | Align the components of a product or service business proposal. | A business idea and knowledge of intended customers and delivery constraints. | Connected business-model elements and assumptions | Facilitator grouping of the source’s four clusters; the source explicitly allows different orders. Validate assumptions outside the workshop. | PDF 128; R |
| Sustainable Business Modelling | Find ways a business can create positive value across its stakeholder network. | Business purpose and knowledge of customers, investors, employees, partners, environment and society. | Stakeholder-value analysis and new value opportunities | The source deliberately places blue-sky ideation after the value analysis. | PDF 130; R |
| Future Visioning | Express a desired future experience grounded in trend research. | Creative trend research and an organisational context. | Communicated vision of a desired future experience | Source recommends subgroups of 3–5; larger teams should divide accordingly. Trend research is a prerequisite, not an outcome invented in the session. | PDF 132; R |
| Ansoff Growth Matrix | Compare growth directions and their organisational risks. | Current products, markets, capabilities and growth ambitions. | Growth directions and their risks | The source places strategic decisions with senior innovation managers; novice designers should not treat the matrix as authority to set company strategy. | PDF 134; R |
| Segmentation-Targeting-Positioning | Choose a target market and articulate a defensible value proposition. | Market evidence, organisational capabilities and competing offers. | Target-market choice and positioning | Market data must precede confident selection. A positioning claim needs an evidence-based reason to believe it. | PDF 136; R |
| VRIO Analysis | Assess resources as potential sources of competitive advantage. | Evidence about organisational resources and capabilities. | Resource assessment and strategic implications | Imitability requires a real understanding of how resources are developed. The matrix is structured and does not replace research. | PDF 138; R |
| Porter's Five Forces | Assess the competitive environment and strategic positioning possibilities. | Evidence about an industry, its geography and relevant actors. | Competitive-environment assessment | Document evidence and scope; judgments depend on which competitive environment was defined. | PDF 140; R |
| Perceptual Map | Compare how customers perceive competing offers. | Relevant product attributes, competitor set and access to potential customers. | Customer-perception map of competing offers | Base the map on customer ratings. Designer guesses do not constitute a measured perceptual map. | PDF 142; R |
| Collage | Make visual qualities or a design vision explicit through a composed image collection. | A purpose such as communicating a vision or deriving visual criteria. | Visual composition and discussed design qualities | A collage’s meaning depends on context and interpretation; discuss its intended message. | PDF 144; R |
| Storyboarding | Make an interaction concept concrete as a sequence of scenes. | An initial idea about users and their interaction with a product or service. | Sequence of interaction scenes | Keep the number of panels proportionate to the message; captions should add information rather than duplicate each image. | PDF 146; R |
| Written Scenario | Describe a use situation that stakeholders can review and improve. | Research about intended users, their goals and a real or intended context. | Contextual narrative for review | Distinguish descriptions of current reality from proposals for an intended future. | PDF 148; R |
| Design Drawing to Define | Communicate agreed design boundaries and starting points visually. | Research conclusions, agreed brief, requirements and open design questions. | Visual boundaries, requirements and open questions | Facilitator grouping of non-numbered guidance. Avoid resolving details prematurely or promising a finished solution through an early illustration. | PDF 150; R |

### Develop & Deliver

| Method | Purpose | Required inputs / access | Expected output | Constraints to check | Source locator / status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Analogies & Metaphors | Transfer useful relationships or expressive qualities from another domain. | A framed problem or intended experiential quality. | Transferred principles or expressive directions | Do not merely copy surface features when using analogies; seek transferable relationships. | PDF 154; R |
| Biomimicry | Use biological strategies to inspire and evaluate design alternatives. | A current system, design challenge and access to reliable biological knowledge. | Biologically inspired design alternatives | Biological research and expert consultation may be necessary before ideation; superficial resemblance is not sufficient. | PDF 156; R |
| Synectics | Use analogy excursions to develop unfamiliar solutions to a framed problem. | A preliminary problem statement and access to the problem owner. | Ideas developed through analogy excursions | The source describes a demanding method that needs experienced facilitation, particularly with inexperienced participants. | PDF 159; R |
| Brainstorming & Brainwriting | Generate, organise and review a broad set of ideas. | A clear concrete challenge framed as a How-To question. | Generated, organised and reviewed ideas | Source participant guidance: 4–15. Protect quieter voices and avoid evaluating during divergence. | PDF 158; R |
| Braindrawing | making ideas visible and developing each other's concepts; typically produces fewer, richer ideas than brainwriting. | a clear concrete target or initial ideas. | Developed sketches for interpretation and selection | reassure hesitant drawers; quality of the drawing is secondary to the communicated idea. | PDF 162; R; starter record |
| Morphological Chart | systematically combining solutions where a clear main function can be divided into sub-functions; requires analytical framing. | main function, sub-functions, and design requirements. | Alternative complete solution concepts | combinations grow rapidly; reduce and group options before combining. For services with no clear main function, choose a less systematic method. | PDF 164; R; starter record |
| SCAMPER | extending or changing existing ideas when initial generation slows. | an existing idea, concept, or product. | Variants of existing ideas | delay criticism of unusual ideas. The source cautions that inexperienced designers may need support. | PDF 166; R; starter record |
| How-Tos | framing a familiar challenge for idea generation; participants need knowledge of the problem and its stakeholders. | short problem statement plus stakeholders and relevant product/service life phases. | Question frames and ideas linked to them | move between concrete and abstract framing when ideas stall; do not let selection of frames erase stakeholder diversity. | PDF 168; R; starter record |
| Harris Profile | making concept evaluations explicit and discussable when requirements exist but precise performance data may not. | comparable concepts and prioritized requirements. | Visual concept comparisons and discussed judgments | define scale meanings per criterion. This is an intuitive visual assessment, not a precise calculation; requirements can evolve during discussion. | PDF 170; R; starter record |
| EVR Decision Matrix | Compare product value and environmental burden against a reference. | Product alternatives, a reference product and value/eco-burden evidence. | Value and eco-burden comparison | Distinguish estimated value and burden from measured evidence; choose and explain the meaning of any additional visual coding. | PDF 172; R |
| C-Box | understanding and evaluating a large early idea set without automatically discarding novelty. | roughly 10–60 ideas on movable notes, according to the source. | Novelty/feasibility map and development choices | proximity on the map does not establish similarity; interesting but difficult ideas may deserve development. | PDF 174; R; starter record |
| vALUe, IR & PMI | Review advantages, limitations and distinctive aspects without killing early ideas. | A manageable set of about 5–10 early ideas or principal solutions. | Early-idea strengths, limitations and development leads | This supports early understanding and development; it is not a final ranking against independent requirements. | PDF 177; R |
| Datum Method | comparing concepts developed to a similar level against explicit criteria. | comparable proposals, criteria, and a chosen reference concept. | Relative concept comparisons and reasoned choice | the method aids decisions rather than mathematically proving them. | Weighted Objectives | selecting among a few developed alternatives with enough information for systematic evaluation. | 3–5 developed alternatives, criteria, weights and enough evidence for scoring. | Weighted comparison and reviewed decision | small numerical differences may not be meaningful; a lower-scoring concept can retain greater development potential. | PDF 180; R; starter record |
| Storytelling | Obtain participant feedback on an idea through a carefully controlled story. | Ideas to evaluate, research questions and intended participants. | Feedback on represented ideas | Rich detail or emotional framing can bias evaluation; stories are representations rather than proof of real-world behaviour. | PDF 182; R |
| Usage Analytics | Develop repeatable analysis of product-use data to inform decisions. | A knowledge need and feasible access to relevant usage data. | Repeatable use-data analysis and implications | Requires data engineering and analysis competence. | Comfort Evaluation | Evaluate comfort and discomfort under realistic use conditions. | Products for comparison, an appropriate sample and a clear evaluation focus. | Context-specific comfort/discomfort findings | Source warns that standard LPD assessment needs at least an hour of exposure to reveal discomfort. | PrEmo & Photo-Elicitation | Understand emotional responses using a pictorial instrument or image-supported interview. | A defined stimulus, participants and the actual PrEmo instrument or photo-elicitation protocol. | Emotional-response evidence; procedure coverage unresolved | Needs verification: the page explains PrEmo and photo-elicitation but does not supply the instrument, response/scoring protocol or a full interview procedure. | Role-Playing | Explore interactions by acting them out and reflecting on the recordings. | An initial interaction idea and intended actor goals. | Recorded interaction exploration and reflections | Role-play informs concepts; designers acting a role do not substitute for evidence from intended users. | PDF 190; R |
| Product Usability Evaluation | Identify usability issues and requirements through observed product use. | A product representation, intended users and realistic use context. | Usability findings and redesign requirements | Research setup and participant recruitment precede testing; informal team review is not equivalent to user evaluation. Numeric sample guidance in the scan is ambiguous and is not encoded as a hard constraint. Results apply to tested functionality only. | PDF 192; R |
| Product Concept Evaluation | Gather structured judgments on concepts to guide selection or improvement. | Concepts, an evaluation purpose and suitable respondents. | Concept judgments and improvement implications | Respondents judge representations of unfinished products. Re-evaluate later as concepts become concrete. | PDF 195; R |
| Cost Price Estimation | Make the assumptions and major cost components of a design explicit. | A sufficiently defined concept, production volume and current cost evidence. | Cost components, assumptions and design implications | Source guidance is grouped editorially. Example prices and taxes are historical; use current project evidence. | PDF 196; R |
| Prototype Reflection Cards | Record what a prototype activity taught and what to do next. | A completed prototype trial and its records. | Recorded learnings and next actions | A prototype trial must precede reflection. Source suggests 10–15 minutes for a card; team discussion is additional. | PDF 198; R |
| Cinematic Prototyping | Explore an interactive object and communicate its use through a short film. | An interactive-object idea and access to making and filming tools. | Film exploring and communicating interaction | Four source phases; fabrication, filming and editing require time beyond planning. | PDF 200; R |
| Design Drawing to Develop | Develop alternatives through iterative visual thinking. | A design challenge or partial idea. | Iterated visual alternatives | Facilitator grouping of non-numbered source guidance. Share unfinished sketches; polishing too early can limit exploration. | PDF 202; R |

## Models, approaches and perspectives

Screen all names in [the catalogue](catalogue.md), keeping these source categories distinct from methods. The method rows above do not turn framework names into workshop procedures.

- **Reasoning in Design** and **Basic Design Cycle**: purpose, components and limitations are recovered in [model records](delft-models-and-drawing.md); follow its links to the original photographs. These are reasoning models, not ready-made synthesis workshops or fixed project phases.
- **Design Drawing as a Language**: recovered perspective and original photograph are linked from the same model records. Select a stage-specific drawing method for executable activity guidance.
- **Product Innovation Process**: PDF position 39 plus the [supplemental diagram](books/delft-supplement/product-innovation-process-diagram.jpg). Inspect the text and diagram before applying it.
- Other catalogue frameworks: discovery names only in this skill. Locate and inspect the actual entry in the bundled scan before claiming its procedure or constraints. They are not silently excluded because a ready-made summary is absent.

## Retrieval pitfalls

- The catalogue alias **WWWH** points to the source heading **WWWWWH**, PDF 102. Preserve the exact source heading when citing it.
- **Synectics** (159) and **Brainstorming & Brainwriting** (158) occur out of printed order. **vALUe, IR & PMI** is at 177; **Experience Prototyping** at 207 and **Wizard of Oz** at 205.
- Catalogue aliases **vALUe, IR & PMI** and **Wizard of Oz** correspond to the source headings **vALUe, IR, and PMI** and **Wizard of Oz Prototyping**. Use the source headings for exact attribution.
- **PrEmo & Photo-Elicitation** is a combined entry describing distinct approaches. Reading PDF 188 does not supply the PrEmo instrument or complete administration/scoring guidance. Inspect what is actually available for the proposed approach; do not invent a combined protocol.
