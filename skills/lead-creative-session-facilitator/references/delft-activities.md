# Selected Delft activities

Source: [Delft scan](books/delft-design-guide.pdf). These are concise paraphrases of visually inspected method pages, not a complete catalogue. PDF page numbers below are 1-based and refer to this saved scan. No exact total duration is given on these pages; estimate the full facilitation footprint separately. Do not attribute estimated group sizes or timings to the book.

## How-Tos — printed p. 175, PDF p. 168

- Fit: framing a familiar challenge for idea generation; participants need knowledge of the problem and its stakeholders.
- Input: short problem statement plus stakeholders and relevant product/service life phases.
- Core: identify stakeholders and aspects; generate multiple action-oriented How-To questions; regroup questions to understand common themes; choose questions spanning perspectives; generate ideas against each chosen question.
- Output: a set of useful question frames and ideas linked to them.
- Consideration: move between concrete and abstract framing when ideas stall; do not let selection of frames erase stakeholder diversity.
- Planning implication (facilitator judgment): allocate time for question generation and selection before the subsequent idea generation. Avoid presenting the complete process as a quick prompt rewrite.

## Braindrawing — printed p. 169, PDF p. 162

- Fit: making ideas visible and developing each other's concepts; typically produces fewer, richer ideas than brainwriting.
- Input: a clear concrete target or initial ideas.
- Source group/material guidance: 4–8 people, A3 sheets, legible dark markers.
- Core: clarify the challenge and divergent rules; each person sketches; pass sheets after a few minutes so others add or create variants; repeat; explain/title the sketches for shared understanding; select promising ideas using criteria.
- Output: developed sketches ready for selection or further design.
- Consideration: reassure hesitant drawers; quality of the drawing is secondary to the communicated idea.
- Planning implication (facilitator judgment): budget each pass, subsequent explanation, and selection. A remote shared-board version is an adaptation and needs explicit turn-taking.

## Morphological Chart — printed p. 171, PDF p. 164

- Fit: systematically combining solutions where a clear main function can be divided into sub-functions; requires analytical framing.
- Input: main function, sub-functions, and design requirements.
- Core: list sub-functions as rows; generate candidate solutions per row; form combinations taking one solution from each row; assess against requirements; choose at least three principal solutions; sketch and elaborate selected combinations.
- Output: alternative complete solution concepts assembled from component choices.
- Consideration: combinations grow rapidly; reduce and group options before combining. For services with no clear main function, choose a less systematic method.
- Planning implication (facilitator judgment): prepare or validate the functional decomposition before expecting participants to produce coherent combinations.

## SCAMPER — printed p. 173, PDF p. 166

- Fit: extending or changing existing ideas when initial generation slows.
- Input: an existing idea, concept, or product.
- Core: explore substitution, combination, adaptation, modification, alternative use, elimination, and reversal; capture variations; regroup for understanding; select promising directions for development.
- Output: new variants or stepping stones from existing concepts.
- Consideration: delay criticism of unusual ideas. The source cautions that inexperienced designers may need support.
- Planning implication (facilitator judgment): demonstrate one prompt and explain that different prompts will yield different amounts of useful material. Selecting only some prompts is an adaptation.

## C-Box — printed p. 181, PDF p. 174

- Fit: understanding and evaluating a large early idea set without automatically discarding novelty.
- Input: roughly 10–60 ideas on movable notes, according to the source.
- Core: define novelty and feasibility axes; review and place ideas together; reflect on gaps; either develop difficult ideas toward feasibility, generate ideas in gaps, or select promising ideas.
- Output: a shared map and a direction for development or selection.
- Consideration: proximity on the map does not establish similarity; interesting but difficult ideas may deserve development.
- Planning implication (facilitator judgment): clarify how feasibility will be judged. Mapping, discussion, and selection are separate time demands. Other axes are an explicitly named adaptation.

## Harris Profile — printed p. 177, PDF p. 170

- Fit: making concept evaluations explicit and discussable when requirements exist but precise performance data may not.
- Input: comparable concepts and prioritized requirements.
- Core: create a four-position scale for each requirement; evaluate each concept; fill a visual profile; present profiles together and discuss implications.
- Output: comparable visual profiles and a reasoned discussion about concepts to explore.
- Consideration: define scale meanings per criterion. This is an intuitive visual assessment, not a precise calculation; requirements can evolve during discussion.
- Planning implication (facilitator judgment): reserve time to agree scale meanings before scoring.

## Datum Method — printed p. 185, PDF p. 178

- Fit: comparing concepts developed to a similar level against explicit criteria.
- Input: comparable proposals, criteria, and a chosen reference concept.
- Core: build a matrix; choose a datum; compare each alternative as worse, same, or better on each criterion; discuss results; use another strong datum and iterate toward a reasoned choice; drop weak designs when appropriate.
- Output: comparisons, clarified strengths/weaknesses, and a selection for development.
- Consideration: the method aids decisions rather than mathematically proving them. Vague criteria can obscure meaningful differences.
- Planning implication (facilitator judgment): budget a bounded number of comparison rounds and protect discussion; do not promise automatic consensus.

## Weighted Objectives — printed p. 187, PDF p. 180

- Fit: selecting among a few developed alternatives with enough information for systematic evaluation.
- Source scope: select 3–5 concepts for evaluation.
- Core: agree criteria and weights; build a matrix; score concepts against criteria; calculate weighted totals; discuss the preferred concept and review weights/scores.
- Output: an explicit weighted comparison supporting a decision.
- Consideration: small numerical differences may not be meaningful; a lower-scoring concept can retain greater development potential.
- Planning implication (facilitator judgment): agree scoring anchors and reserve discussion for close results. Calculate weighted sums consistently rather than treating rankings as objective proof.
